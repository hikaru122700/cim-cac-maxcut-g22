# Stage 17: Paper Draft

## Title

**WARP: Warm-Starting Physics-Inspired Ising Solvers for Anytime MAX-CUT Optimization**

## Abstract

Coherent Ising Machines (CIM) and Chaotic Amplitude Control (CAC) are increasingly promoted as fast MAX-CUT solvers, yet their advantage over well-tuned classical heuristics is rarely established on common ground: published comparisons mix solver-internal cut counts, omit wall-clock cost, and leave classical baselines under-tuned. Whether physics-inspired dynamics are best deployed as standalone optimizers, or in combination with classical search, remains unquantified. We introduce WARP, a unified anytime benchmark that scores every solver with one shared weighted-cut function on a common wall-clock axis, paired with two combination strategies: a warm-start hybrid that hands a short physics run to a classical refiner, and a parallel portfolio. Across four MAX-CUT instances spanning sparse, dense, weighted, and large-scale regimes, tuned classical solvers reach within 0.01% of best-known cuts in under one second, whereas CIM rises quickly but plateaus short of the optimum and collapses on a dense instance when its parameters are not re-tuned. Crucially, **warm-starting Tabu Search from a brief CIM run breaks this plateau to within a single cut of the best-known value and reduces CIM's gap on the dense instance by roughly 85%**, dominating cold-start at matched refinement budget. No single solver wins on every instance, motivating parallel portfolios for robustness.

## 1. Introduction

MAX-CUT is among the most studied NP-hard combinatorial optimization problems, asking for a bipartition of a graph's vertices that maximizes the total weight of edges crossing the partition. Its importance extends well beyond graph theory: MAX-CUT is the canonical zero-field Ising ground-state problem, and a large family of scheduling, circuit-layout, and statistical-physics tasks reduce to it (Barahona et al., 1988; Lucas, 2014). This dual identity has made MAX-CUT the de facto benchmark for an emerging class of *physics-inspired* solvers that emulate analog dynamical systems. Coherent Ising Machines exploit the bistable phase of degenerate optical parametric oscillators to relax toward low-energy spin configurations (McMahon et al., 2016; Inoue & Yoshida, 2022); Simulated Bifurcation (SB) discretizes the adiabatic evolution of a network of nonlinear oscillators (Goto et al., 2019; 2021); and Chaotic Amplitude Control (CAC) augments such dynamics with error feedback that equalizes oscillator amplitudes (Leleu et al., 2021). These methods routinely report striking time-to-solution figures and have driven interest in dedicated hardware accelerators (Mohseni et al., 2022).

Despite this momentum, the literature offers surprisingly little that lets a practitioner answer a basic question: *for a fixed wall-clock budget, which solver returns the best cut, and is a physics-inspired solver worth using at all?* Three methodological gaps recur. First, physics-machine evaluations frequently report solver-internal cut counts that ignore edge weights or use machine-native time units, making cross-solver comparison apples-to-oranges (Tiunov et al., 2019; Kalinin & Berloff, 2018). Second, classical baselines such as simulated annealing (Kirkpatrick et al., 1983) or tabu search (Glover, 1989) are often included as strawmen rather than tuned competitors, even though state-of-the-art memetic algorithms remain the strongest known MAX-CUT heuristics on standard instances (Wu & Hao, 2013; Benlic & Hao, 2013). Third, while it is widely *asserted* that physics dynamics and classical local search are complementary, the value of combining them is rarely measured under a matched budget on a common clock — in contrast to the carefully controlled warm-start studies emerging in the quantum-optimization community (Egger et al., 2021). Without addressing these gaps, claims about physics-inspired superiority rest on incomparable measurements.

We close these gaps with WARP, a framework built on a single principle: every solver is judged by one shared weighted-cut score, recomputed from the spins it returns, along a unified wall-clock axis. Around this protocol we tune all six solvers per instance so that each is shown near its best, then study two ways of *combining* them. The first is a warm-start hybrid that runs a physics explorer (CIM or CAC) for a short fixed budget, takes its spin configuration as an initializer, and hands it to a classical refiner (tabu search or warm-start annealing); the total time charged is explorer plus refiner, and the hybrid is compared against a random-initialized refiner at *matched* refinement budget. The central hypothesis is that a brief physics run is a better-than-random *global initializer* whose characteristic plateau is then broken by classical hill-climbing.
