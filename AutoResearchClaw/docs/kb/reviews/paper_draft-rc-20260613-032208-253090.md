# Stage 17: Paper Draft

## Title

**DiPS: Do Learned Pump Schedules Beat a Sigmoid Ramp in CIMs?**

## Abstract

The coherent Ising machine (CIM) solves MAX-CUT by annealing a network of optical parametric oscillators, and the single most consequential design choice in that annealing is the pump-power schedule p(t) that drives the bifurcation mapping oscillator amplitudes onto spins. In almost all CIM practice this schedule is fixed by hand to a linear or sigmoid ramp, and the few studies that tune it adjust only a handful of scalars of an assumed shape, leaving open whether the *shape itself* is a worthwhile optimization target. DiPS (Differentiable Pump Scheduling) recasts p(t) as the parameters of a differentiable rollout of the CIM dynamics and optimizes its full shape by backpropagation through the simulated trajectory, benchmarked under a strictly matched function-evaluation budget against scalar, analytic, gradient-free, monotonic, and instance-conditioned alternatives. Across eight schedule and solver conditions, optimizing a low-dimensional scalar ramp already cuts the best-of-20 optimality gap by **nearly 70% relative to the hand-set linear baseline**, while high-capacity freeform and gradient-learned schedules match this to within roughly one percentage point rather than surpassing it. Instance conditioning on graph spectra underperforms simpler schedules. The dominant residual lever is feedback control of the dynamics, not the open-loop pump shape, redirecting where tuning effort should go.

## Introduction

Physical and simulated Ising machines have emerged as a leading route to fast, energy-efficient approximate solutions of combinatorial optimization problems, with MAX-CUT serving as the canonical benchmark [mohseni2022ising]. Among these, the coherent Ising machine realizes spins as the phases of degenerate optical parametric oscillators in a measurement-feedback network, and it has been scaled to one hundred thousand spins on real hardware [honjo2021spin, yamamoto2020coherent]. The behavior of such a machine is governed by how its gain is ramped through the oscillation threshold: the pump-power schedule p(t) sets the rate at which the network crosses the bifurcation that selects a spin configuration, and recent analyses show that this bifurcation structure is precisely what determines whether the continuous dynamics lands in a good cut or a poor local fixed point [wang2023bifurcation, goto2021highperformance]. The schedule is therefore not a peripheral hyperparameter but the central control surface separating a competitive solver from a stalled one.

Despite this centrality, the pump schedule is treated remarkably conservatively. The overwhelmingly common choice is a monotonic linear or sigmoid ramp from below to above threshold, and even careful engineering efforts adjust only the initial gain, final gain, and ramp rate—a low-dimensional slice of an assumed shape [hamerly2020synchronouslypumped, kako2020coherent]. A separate line of work derives schedules from Kibble–Zurek scaling arguments about critical crossing rates [cui2020experimentally], while general-purpose hyperparameter search optimizes the same few scalars with Bayesian or evolutionary methods [bischl2023hyperparameter, greenhill2020bayesian]. What has not been tested is whether p(t) treated as a *free, high-dimensional functional object*—a piecewise or network-parameterized curve, possibly conditioned on the instance—yields cuts that a near-trivial scalar ramp cannot already reach. Compounding this gap, head-to-head comparisons across schedule families rarely equalize the search effort spent per family, so a richer schedule can appear better simply because it consumed more function evaluations rather than because its shape is genuinely superior [bartzbeielstein2020benchmarking, oshiyama2022benchmark].

The opportunity to close this gap is methodological. The mean-field CIM dynamics form a system of ordinary differential equations that integrate cheaply and are fully differentiable, so the gradient of the cut value with respect to the schedule parameters can be obtained by backpropagating through the simulated rollout—the same machinery that has reshaped differentiable physics and learning-to-optimize [wang2023pair, mazyavkina2021reinforcement]. We exploit this to build **DiPS** (Differentiable Pump Scheduling), a framework that expresses p(t)—and, in its strongest variant, the feedback gains of chaotic amplitude control—as parameters of a differentiable rollout and trains them end-to-end by gradient descent. Crucially, DiPS pairs every learned schedule with a gradient-free twin, an analytic anchor, and a scalar reference inside one harness, and evaluates them all under a single function-evaluation budget on the G-set MAX-CUT instances. This design lets us separate two axes that prior work conflates: the *solver* axis (the dynamics and any feedback) and the *schedule* axis (the shape of the open-loop pump).
