# Stage 17: Paper Draft

## Title

**PACE: When Tuned Heuristics Outrun Ising Machines on MAX-CUT**

---

## Abstract

Coherent Ising Machines and Chaotic Amplitude Control are widely promoted as superior MAX-CUT solvers, yet the head-to-head studies behind these claims typically pit a heavily optimized physics solver against classical heuristics left at literature-default settings — an unfair protocol that inflates the apparent physics advantage. A credible comparison must tune every contender, score them identically, and report solution quality as a function of wall-clock time. PACE is a fair anytime evaluation framework that tunes all contending solvers — CIM, CAC, simulated annealing, simulated bifurcation, parallel tempering with isoenergetic cluster moves, and a newly implemented memetic genetic algorithm — per instance with Optuna, scores them with one identical weighted-cut function on a shared time axis, and composes them into warm-start hybrids and parallel portfolios. Across four standard G-set and Sherrington–Kirkpatrick benchmarks, tuned classical heuristics dominate: the memetic algorithm reaches the best-known cut **exactly (zero gap) on G22** and leads the dense weighted instance, while simulated bifurcation is the most reliable solver, staying within roughly 0.2% of the best-known cut on average. Fair tuning alone lifts annealing from hopeless to mid-pack. Warm-starting Tabu Search from a physics run breaks its quality plateau, cutting one solver's gap by about 85%, yet never beats the best tuned single solver — positioning physics-inspired dynamics as explorers and portfolio members rather than standalone solvers.

## Introduction

MAX-CUT is among the most studied NP-hard combinatorial problems: given a weighted graph, the goal is to partition its vertices into two sets so that the total weight of edges crossing the partition is maximized (Karp, 1972). Beyond its theoretical centrality — it admits the celebrated 0.878-approximation via semidefinite relaxation (Goemans & Williamson, 1995) — MAX-CUT has become the de facto benchmark for an entire generation of physics-inspired optimization hardware and algorithms. Coherent Ising Machines (CIM) encode the problem in the steady state of coupled optical parametric oscillators (McMahon et al., 2016; Inagaki et al., 2016), Chaotic Amplitude Control (CAC) augments such dynamics with error-correcting auxiliary variables to escape local traps (Leleu et al., 2021), and Simulated Bifurcation (SB) numerically integrates an adiabatic Hamiltonian on commodity processors (Goto et al., 2019, 2021). Because these methods promise to deliver high-quality cuts in a fixed, often very short, time budget, the practically relevant metric is not the eventual optimum but the *anytime* trajectory: how good a cut can be returned at each wall-clock instant. This time-to-quality framing is what ultimately decides whether a solver is useful in a deployment loop.

The literature that motivates physics-inspired hardware, however, rests on comparisons that are methodologically fragile. Three recurring problems undermine the standard "physics beats classical" narrative. First, the physics solver is usually tuned extensively — its couplings, pump schedules, and step sizes optimized per instance — while the classical opponents (simulated annealing, parallel tempering, genetic algorithms) are run at textbook default parameters, so the contest compares an optimized method against an un-optimized one (Hamerly et al., 2019; Leleu et al., 2021). Second, cut quality is often measured inconsistently: internal spin counters that ignore edge weights are perfectly adequate for unweighted G-set graphs but silently misreport quality on weighted Sherrington–Kirkpatrick instances. Third, results are reported as a single final cut rather than as a wall-clock trajectory, which hides the fact that a fast-rising solver may plateau well below a slower competitor (Aramon et al., 2019; Mohseni et al., 2022). Taken together, these practices leave a clear methodological hole: there exists no uniformly tuned, uniformly scored, anytime comparison of physics-inspired and classical MAX-CUT solvers. Without one, claims of physical advantage cannot be separated from tuning asymmetry.

To close this gap we introduce **PACE** (Physics-And-Classical anytime Evaluation), a fairness-first benchmark and composition harness. The central methodological move is parity: every solver — CIM, CAC, simulated annealing (SA), simulated bifurcation (SB), parallel tempering with isoenergetic cluster moves (PT-ICM), and a memetic genetic algorithm (GA) we implement specifically as a strong missing classical baseline — is tuned per instance with the same Optuna budget and the same objective. A single weighted-cut scorer is applied to the raw spin output of every solver, so weighted and unweighted instances are treated correctly and identically.
