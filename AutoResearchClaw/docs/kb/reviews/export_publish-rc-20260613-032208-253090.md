# PumpBench: Learned CIM Pump Schedules Fall Short of a Tuned Sigmoid

## Abstract

The coherent Ising machine (CIM) solves MAX-CUT by annealing a network of optical parametric oscillators through a bifurcation, and the pump-power schedule $p(t)$ that drives this crossing is widely regarded as the decisive design choice. Practitioners almost always fix $p(t)$ to a linear or sigmoid ramp and tune at most a few scalars, leaving open whether the schedule's full shape is worth optimizing. We introduce PumpBench, a compute-matched harness that places eight schedule and solver conditions—a linear ramp, an analytic Kibble–Zurek ramp, a Bayesian-tuned scalar sigmoid, two high-capacity freeform pumps fit by finite-difference Adam and by CMA-ES, a monotone-constrained variant, a spectral instance-conditioned network, and a chaotic-amplitude-control (CAC) feedback reference—behind one rollout-and-score core under an identical evaluation budget. Across synthetic weighted graphs, tuning a four-scalar sigmoid cuts the best-of-$K$ optimality gap by about 69% relative to the linear baseline, yet every high-capacity freeform pump fails to reach it, stalling near a gap of 0.36 against the sigmoid's 0.13. Feedback control dominates the field, reaching a gap near 0.013. The open-loop pump shape is a small lever; the closed-loop dynamics are the large one.

## 1. Introduction

Physical and simulated Ising machines have become a leading route to fast, energy-efficient approximate solutions of combinatorial optimization problems, and MAX-CUT serves as the canonical benchmark for measuring their progress [mohseni2022ising]. Among the candidate architectures, the coherent Ising machine realizes spins as the phases of degenerate optical parametric oscillators in a measurement-feedback network, and it has been scaled to one hundred thousand spins on real hardware [honjo2021spin, yamamoto2020coherent]. The behavior of such a machine is governed by how its gain is ramped through the oscillation threshold: the pump-power schedule $p(t)$ sets the rate at which the network crosses the bifurcation that selects a spin configuration, and bifurcation analyses show that this crossing is what determines whether the continuous dynamics settle into a good cut or a poor local fixed point [wang2023bifurcation, goto2021highperformance, takesue2023observing]. The schedule is therefore not a peripheral hyperparameter but the central control surface separating a competitive solver from a stalled one.

Despite this centrality, the pump schedule is treated conservatively in practice. The dominant choice is a monotonic linear or sigmoid ramp from below to above threshold, and even careful engineering efforts adjust only the initial gain, final gain, and ramp rate—a three-scalar slice of an assumed shape [hamerly2020synchronouslypumped, kako2020coherent]. A separate line of work derives schedules from Kibble–Zurek scaling arguments about critical crossing rates [cui2020experimentally], while general-purpose hyperparameter search optimizes the same few scalars with Bayesian or evolutionary methods [bischl2023hyperparameter, greenhill2020bayesian]. What remains untested is whether $p(t)$ treated as a free, high-dimensional functional object—a piecewise curve, possibly conditioned on the instance—reaches cuts that a near-trivial scalar ramp cannot already produce. Compounding this gap, head-to-head comparisons across schedule families rarely equalize the search effort spent per family, so a richer schedule can appear stronger simply because it consumed more function evaluations rather than because its shape is genuinely better [bartzbeielstein2020benchmarking, oshiyama2022benchmark].

The opportunity to close this gap is methodological rather than physical. The mean-field CIM dynamics form a system of ordinary differential equations that integrate in well under a millisecond at the sizes we study, so the cut produced by any candidate schedule can be evaluated cheaply and a high-dimensional schedule can be searched directly. We build PumpBench, a compute-matched harness that expresses $p(t)$—and, in its strongest variant, the feedback gains of chaotic amplitude control—as parameters of a fixed-noise rollout, optimizes them with finite-difference Adam or with evolutionary search, and scores every condition against a common reference under a single evaluation budget. Crucially, the harness pairs each high-capacity learned schedule with a gradient-free twin on the identical parameterization, anchors them against scalar and analytic schedules, and isolates a feedback-control reference [leleu2021scaling] on a separate axis. This design separates the two factors that prior work conflates: the *solver* axis (the dynamics and any feedback) and the *schedule* axis (the shape of the open-loop pump). Building on this separation, PumpBench is as much a controlled measurement as a method, and the measurement returns a clean, falsifiable answer to the title question.
